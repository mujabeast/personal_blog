title: My Cybersecurity Journey
description: A chronological account of how I moved from first exposure, to rebuilding the fundamentals, to creating challenges and teaching beginners.
---

My route into cybersecurity has not been a straight progression from one skill to the next. It has been a repeated cycle: try something, notice what I do not understand, return to the foundations, and build again with a little more care.

## Before NTU

My first formal exposure came through **EE5084 Cybersecurity** during GEM Trailblazer. It gave me an initial view of the field, but cybersecurity was not yet a fully developed personal direction. I had encountered the subject; I had not yet built the habits or technical foundations needed to pursue it seriously.

That distinction matters. Being interested in security is different from doing the patient work of learning operating systems, networks and software behaviour.

## June to August 2025: starting from the foundations

On **25 June 2025**, I began learning Linux through OverTheWire Bandit. The challenges gave me a practical reason to use the shell and to become comfortable with files, permissions, processes and small pieces of problem solving.

I continued with beginner material from HTB Academy, including the fundamentals of the penetration-testing process. The most useful change was not learning a single technique; it was beginning to see security work as a method: define scope, enumerate carefully, form hypotheses, test them and document the result.

On **5 July 2025**, I attended the NUS GreyCTF Summit. I also used TryHackMe Premium and, on **13 August 2025**, attended my first NTU cybersecurity community meetup. These activities gave me more routes into the field, but I was still collecting foundations rather than specialising.

## January to March 2026: returning more seriously

By early 2026 I had to admit that parts of my networking knowledge were weaker than I wanted. On **4 January 2026**, I started rebuilding those foundations through Cisco Academy.

Returning to basic networking was a correction, not a detour. It made later web testing, enumeration and traffic analysis easier to reason about because I was less dependent on memorised steps.

As I became more involved in the local cybersecurity community, I also began moving from passive attendance towards contribution. On **11 January 2026**, I presented an early SentCTF concept at a Sentinels retreat. On **19 March 2026**, I led a beginner cybersecurity event and taught OSINT.

Teaching exposed a different kind of gap. A technique that feels familiar is not necessarily something I can explain clearly. Designing a beginner-friendly path forced me to think about sequence, assumptions and the ethical boundaries around open-source research.

## March to June 2026: from learning to building

During March and April 2026, I created and documented **NTU Rewind**, an OSINT CTF challenge based on public historical and location-based sources. It became an early milestone in my shift from solving a challenge to designing one.

Challenge creation changed the questions I asked. A solver can sometimes move past an awkward clue; a creator has to consider fairness, reliability, unintended paths and what the challenge is actually teaching.

In May, I expanded my web-security study through PortSwigger Web Security Academy. I continued building pentesting notes and learning SOC concepts. Competition participation gave me reasons to apply the material under constraints, while authorised bug bounty testing between **8 June and 10 July 2026** required a more careful workflow: remain inside scope, keep evidence, avoid unnecessary impact and report responsibly.

In **June 2026**, I qualified for the TISC and CDDC finals. I treat those as milestones in the journey rather than evidence that the fundamentals are complete.

## June to July 2026: from participant to leader

My responsibilities within NTU Sentinels grew during June and July. I began coordinating more technical contributors, helping create educational opportunities and learning the less visible work behind a safe university cybersecurity community.

That work includes governance, privacy and sponsor responsibility. Those constraints are not separate from technical work. They shape what can be collected, published and demonstrated, and they are part of designing events people can trust.

At the same time, I began exploring physical, IoT and hardware challenge development. In July I moved deeper into Linux program loading, ELF files, assembly, binary exploitation and reverse-engineering foundations. These areas have made it clear how much more there is to learn.

## Current direction

My developing strengths include Linux and networking, web and API security, OSINT, Burp-based manual testing, enumeration methodology, responsible bug bounty workflow, beginner teaching, CTF challenge creation and technical community leadership.

I am still deliberately improving reverse engineering, binary exploitation, memory forensics, Active Directory, mobile security, advanced API testing, firmware and hardware security, technical writing and AI security.

The aim of this site is not to turn that list into a personal brand. It is to make the process inspectable: what changed, what I built, what I misunderstood and what I plan to study next.
